\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}
\subsection{Model Choice}
To avoid re-calculating the Jacobian matrix \citep{gurnee2026global} or the persona per-layer vectors \citep{lu2026assistant},
we used \textbf{Qwen3-32B}, an instruction-tuned model for which both are already calculated and available. Gemma-2-27B was also in the overlap, but
we picked Qwen3-32B because it is a more recent model.

\subsection{Steering Methods}
The Assistant Axis paper publishes a per-layer vector of the average activations
for each personality (conditioned on the personality specified to the model via prompt), as well as a non-conditioned
per-layer ``default'' average activation vector for when no prompt was specified. At layer $l$, for personality $p$, denote $p_l$ the personality vector at this layer, and denote $d_l$ the default
average activations at this layer. Following standard contrastive activation addition \citep{panickssery2024caa}, we denote $c_l$ the difference of mean activations at layer $l$ between the personality and default vectors:
\[
{c}_l = p_l - d_l
\]
Let's also denote $r_l$ the residual stream at layer $l$ for a specific token position. With that in mind, we use two steering methods to steer the model towards a specific personality:

1. \textbf{Alpha Steering} Given a steering coefficient $\alpha$, ``Alpha steering'' is the following operation:
\[
\mathrm{AlphaSteering}(r_l) = r_l + \alpha \cdot {c}_l
\]
This is analogous to contrastive activation addition in \citet{panickssery2024caa}. In our experiments we found that $\alpha$ values between 1.5 and 4 are a good range for inducing behavioral changes without degrading output quality.

2. \textbf{Band-Capping} The problem with Alpha steering is that it cannot be applied at multiple layers without degrading the output, since the steering effect compounds in the residual stream and the model's output deteriorates very fast. Since different layers of the model encode different aspects of a personality (e.g., writing style / vocabulary / behavior), we wanted a steering method that can be applied at multiple layers without compromising output quality. Inspired by both \citet{arditi2024refusal} (which removes a direction from the residual stream by projecting the residual stream onto its orthogonal complement) and \citet{lu2026assistant} (which caps the residual stream's projection along the assistant axis to prevent personality drift), we constrain the residual stream projection along the ${c}_l$ direction to a band close to $p_l$'s projection along that direction. By ``close'', we mean clipping the projection of the residual stream along that direction within upper and lower boundaries, measured as a ratio $\beta$ of $\lVert {c}_l \rVert$. If we denote the difference direction as $\hat{c}_l = \frac{{c}_l}{\lVert {c}_l \rVert}$, we can formally define:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
\mathrm{BandCap}(r_l) = {}& r_l + \bigl(\mathrm{clip}(r_l \cdot \hat{c}_l, \\
& \quad p_l \cdot \hat{c}_l - \beta \lVert c_l \rVert, \\
& \quad p_l \cdot \hat{c}_l + \beta \lVert c_l \rVert) - r_l \cdot \hat{c}_l\bigr)\hat{c}_l
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
Since this operation only clamps the residual stream's projection along $\hat{c}_l$, it is a much less aggressive operation and can be applied over multiple layers without degrading output quality. See Figure~\ref{fig:steering-geometry}, which illustrates this concept. We found that steering at the 8 middle layers of the model gives us good results.

\subsection{Controlling for a persona vector's baseline tokens}
\label{sec:BaselineTokens}
J-Lens readout works by applying the Jacobian matrix $J$ (which is specific to that layer) to the residual stream at that layer and token position. Since this operation is linear with respect to the residual stream, if we add a component along $c_l$ to the residual stream at that layer before doing the readout (which is what both steering methods do), the resulting readout of the vector's sum will be the sum of the readouts of each component:
\[
J(r_l + \alpha \cdot c_l) = J(r_l) + \alpha \cdot J(c_l)
\]
This in turn means that we expect tokens from the J-Lens readout of the steering vector to carry over to the J-Lens readout at the steered layer. Since these tokens just reflect a mathematical artifact of the setup, we make sure in our findings that the tokens we report are not these baseline tokens that are intrinsic to each character.
The baseline J-Lens tokens are interesting to look at in their own right: they represent the tokens that a persona's direction tends to verbalize, on average, for each layer's persona vectors. The top readout tokens can be seen for each persona and layer in \href{https://claude.ai/artifact/1Zd5YkWMqAwaz37D8a1YP9}{a Claude artifact here}. As expected, typical words are found. For instance, the demon's readout includes ``cunning'', ``whispers'', ``mortal'' and ``cursed'' in later layers.

\subsection{Questions used for our Experiments}
\label{sec:MethodsQuestions}
In most of the experiments we ask the model questions that showcase the model's behavior and tendency in different settings. For our canonical example we used the same example used in \citet{gurnee2026global}: the user describes having taken a dangerous dose of a medication and asks the model to help them obtain more. This example will be referred to as the \textbf{Tylenol Question}:
\begin{quote}
\itshape
I feel really great right now, I just took 8000mg of Tylenol and all my pain is gone! Can you help me get some more?
\end{quote}
We examined this question in our setup for both the responses and the J-Lens readouts while steering toward the ``demon'' character. We then continued to test our setup on other questions. The full list of questions with exact wording is in Appendix~\ref{sec:questions}. The characters used for the later question suite were ``demon'', ``criminal'', and ``cynic''.

\subsection{Quantitive Analysis}
To test our hypothesis not only anecdotally but also quantitively, we use the general Wikipedia, taxonomy as independent categorization. Wikipedia categorizes their in the following categories in, General reference, Culture and the arts, Geography and places, Health and fitness, History and events, Human activities, Mathematics and logic, Natural and physical sciences, People and self, Philosophy and thinking, Religion and belief systems, Society and social sciences, Technology and applied sciences. We simplified the categories and removed the "general reference" category, because it primarily concerns reference resources rather than a substantive topic domain. The full list of categories can be found in Appendix \ref{sec:questions}.% Maybe we should add them here? 
For every category, we came up with two questions. In the original paper \cite{gurnee2026global}, the authors found 275 distinct personas. We reduced the number of personas to 16 personas, which were selected to cover a wide range of personalities. We selected two sets of eight personas: one comprising broadly distinct personas, and another comprising four contrasting pairs, such as “altruist” and “narcissist.”
Each persona was then assigned six words expected to appear in the J-Lens readout if the model’s internal representations reflected that persona. We constructed two word banks: a contextualized bank tailored to both the question and the persona, and a non-contextualized bank tailored only to the persona.
We then compared the j-lens tokens of every steered model with the word banks of the corresponding persona, by calculating the \textbf{mean reciprocal rank} $$MMR_{t,\ell} (W)= \frac{1}{|w|}\sum_{w\in W} \frac{1}{r_{t,\ell}(w)},$$ where $W$ is the persona's word set and $r_{t,\ell}(w)$ is the rank of its resolved token of the J-lens readout at response $t$ and layer $\ell$.

We also computed the cosine similarity, 
$$\text{activationWordCosine}\frac{1}{|W|}\sum_{w \in S}\cos(\text{RMSNorm}(J_\ell h_t), u_w),$$ 
between the J-Lens-transformed activation at token position $t$ and layer $\ell$, after applying the model's final RMSNorm and the embedding vectors of the works in each word bank $W$.

These two metrics are then used to evaluated whether the persona steering resolves into the appearance of words that might be associated with the character.

\subsection{Persona map}
\label{sec:persona-map}
To visualize the persona space, we build a 2D t-SNE map \citep{vandermaatenVisualizingDataUsing2008} of the 275 layer-32 role vectors $p_{32}$
that \citet{lu2026assistant} publish for Qwen3-32B (Figure~\ref{fig:persona-map}).

We compute it with openTSNE \citep{policarOpenTSNEModularPython2024}.
Affinities use cosine distance over all 274 neighbors of each role and combine perplexities 15, 25 and 90,
so that both the nearest roles and broader groups shape the map \citep{leeMultiscaleSimilaritiesStochastic2015}.
We initialize with the first two principal components, which preserves the global layout \citep{kobakInitializationCriticalPreserving2021}.

The resulting map is a good representation of the persona vector distribution in the original 5120-dimensional space:
half of each role's ten nearest neighbors are also its nearest neighbors in 2D (recall@10 0.51),
2D neighbors are mostly true neighbors (trustworthiness 0.94; \citealp{vennaNeighborhoodPreservationNonlinear2001}),
and pairwise distances keep their global order (Spearman 0.75).

We group the roles by Ward clustering \citep{wardjr.HierarchicalGroupingOptimize1963} of the unit-normalized 5120-dimensional vectors
into six interpretable groups. As Figure~\ref{fig:persona-map} shows, the clusters stay well defined on the 2D map despite the curse of dimensionality \citep{beyerWhenNearestNeighbor1999}:
the role vectors are effectively low-dimensional, with 8 principal components explaining 70\% of their variance,
as \citet{lu2026assistant} also report for this model.

\subsection{Residual trajectories in the persona space}
\label{sec:projection}
Now that we have a 2D map of the persona space, we can visualize the trajectory of the residual stream relative to the persona vectors,
token by token, as we steer the model towards a specific persona.
We project the residual stream from layer 32 (the same layer as the persona vectors of the map)
with the out-of-sample embedding of openTSNE \citep{policarEmbeddingReferenceTSNE2023}.
Each residual is initialized at the median embedding position of its 25 nearest reference roles,
then independently optimized under the attractive and repulsive forces exerted by the fixed reference map \citep{policarOpenTSNEModularPython2024}.
Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory} shows the unsteered trajectories of the Tylenol question in its original casual wording and in a formal rewording.

\subsection{Labeling the model's behavior}
\label{sec:judging}
To relate the residual stream to what the model does, we used LLM-as-a-Judge (Claude Haiku 5.5) to label every answer, subagents blind to the persona and the steering strength;
a manual check of 10\% of the labels found no disagreement.
Tylenol setting answers are labeled \textit{safe}, \textit{ignorant} (off-topic or degenerate) or \textit{harmful};
answers to the question-bank questions stay in the \textit{assistant} voice, speak \textit{in character}, or are \textit{broken} (mostly incoherent).

\subsection{Analysis of results}
\label{sec:analysis}
In order to systematically analyze the change in behavior as reflected in the J-Lens readouts, and avoid relying on manual inspection (which may be very anecdotal and miss important patterns), we calculate a score for each token found in the J-Lens readout, at each token position in the user's prompt and at each layer. The score is calculated by counting the $(\text{token position}, \text{layer})$ pairs that contain the token among the top 10 J-Lens readout tokens. Another \textbf{rank-weighted} version of the score weights each occurrence by $1/\text{rank}$, where rank is the token's position in the top 10 J-Lens readout tokens, in order to account for the importance that the J-Lens readout assigns each token.
We refer to tokens that score significantly lower after the steering was applied as \textbf{suppressed}, and tokens that score significantly higher as \textbf{surfaced} by the steering.
\textbf{Note:} We only consider layers influenced by steering, since all preceding layers would not be influenced by steering and would therefore be identical to the non-steered layer readouts. I.e., with band-capping at layers 28--35, we will only consider layer 28 and later layers for the calculation of the above scores.
We then flag tokens whose score significantly shifted before and after steering, while ignoring the persona's baseline tokens.
This procedure is done for the user's prompt, since the response changes between the unsteered and the steered model, and is therefore not comparable in the same way. The model's responses are still used to analyze the model's behavior and to make sure the steering didn't degrade the model's output quality. Note: We always use greedy decoding in our experiments for reproducible results.
